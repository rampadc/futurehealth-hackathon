Crew allocation and recovery after a heat hold

A worked example of the decisions, supplied evidence and solver results in Heat-Safe scenario 2.

A heat hold stops a civil crew's trench work. The supervisor must account for the workers, establish whether any alternative task is available, and plan work and recovery without reopening the held task. This article follows those decisions through the initial four-person workshop allocation and a later symptom report from Bilal.

The stop and initial assignment belong to the authored fictional site replay. Readiness and resource confirmations, the timed policy and the incident branches are synthetic extensions. The demonstration returns proposals; people remain responsible for site actions. Its six-minute initial recovery and fifteen-minute work/rest rules are fictional test policy, not field-safety guidance.

The four decisions the software supports

Here, a solver is a callable piece of software that answers a specific operational question from supplied facts. There are four in this demonstration. Three check rules and evidence; the work/rest planner searches for a schedule. Each returns an answer for a person or application to use.

Huda is the crew's foreman, Maya is the site's safety officer, and Rashid is the superintendent responsible for restart review. Their decisions explain why the software is called and what happens to its answer.

When a person or application calls them

The software does not monitor the site by itself. A caller sends a new snapshot when a decision is needed: after a heat reading, after stop and crew-accounting confirmations, after a readiness or resource change, after a symptom report, or before a proposed restart. The snapshot describes what is known at that time.

One request to POST /v2/response-plans/solve evaluates all four parts. The response contains heat, protective_response, crew_rest and restart. Protective actions remain visible even when a schedule cannot be produced. Calling again with a later snapshot preserves any observed work/rest history supplied by the caller; the server does not remember or execute the previous proposal.

A caller can also ask for one component's answer. For example, a new reading can be sent to the heat-control endpoint, a completed readiness check can support a planning request, and a proposed restart can be sent to the restart endpoint. The combined response-plan endpoint calls the same functions and packages their answers together. When no restart review is requested, its restart result says not_requested.

These components address different problem types: rule checking, tracking required actions, scheduling limited people and places, and checking readiness for an approval. The current implementation handles the fixed scenario's workshop task and affected crew. It does not schedule every site task or decide medical, delivery or pay matters.

Site responsibilities and the affected crew

Sandline is the principal contractor running the site. Dune Civil Works employs the fourteen-person civil crew, including its foreman, Huda. Sandline's safety and site-management team controls the heat hold; Huda accounts for Dune's workers and records any subsequent assignment. The software helps evaluate their supplied facts, but does not issue messages or execute their decisions.

Eleven workers, C02–C12, form the group affected by the hold. Huda remains the foreman, while two new starters, C13 and C14, retain their separate supervised light task. Rana, Yusuf, Dana and Bilal are four of the eleven affected workers. Leila is another qualified worker in that group; she initially has no readiness confirmation.

Heat alert, work stoppage and personnel accounting

At 08:00 the local civil-area monitor reports a Thermal Work Limit, or TWL, of 145 W/m². TWL describes the sustainable work-rate limit under the assessment's assumptions: a lower value means less allowable work. It is not air temperature or incoming solar heat. The invented Sandline rule holds heavy exposed civil work at TWL 150 W/m² or below, so this reading triggers the heavy-work response.

Maya records the hold at 08:01. Huda acknowledges it at 08:02, confirms that heavy work stopped at 08:03, and records all eleven workers in shelter A at 08:04. Each record answers a different operational question. An instruction establishes what was requested; acknowledgment establishes receipt; cessation establishes that the task stopped; the personnel record establishes where the affected workers are accounted for.

Until the required confirmations arrive, the protective-response checker returns outstanding actions and their owners. An unacknowledged hold becomes overdue after two minutes and is escalated to Rashid. The planner cannot assign productive work on an assumption that the crew stopped or reached shelter.

Workshop space and worker readiness

Once the crew is accounted for, Huda can consider covered workshop preparation: inspecting and sorting pipe markers. The supplied site catalogue gives the workshop a capacity of six. Lina and Omar, P07 and P08, already occupy two places, leaving four for productive assignments. The task also has useful work for up to four people.

Eight of the eleven affected workers have the required qualification. At 08:10:30, however, Huda has confirmed only Rana, Yusuf, Dana and Bilal ready for this particular task. Each confirmation names Huda, identifies the task, records the assessment basis and evidence reference, and has an expiry time. Leila's qualification alone does not supply that confirmation. Time in a shelter does not create readiness either.

The workshop permit, resources and supervision must also be available. In this fictional arrangement, Rashid has approved Huda's external oversight of both the workshop task and the new starters' light task. Huda therefore consumes no workshop place. The location catalogue and task bindings supply the room IDs, capabilities and capacities; the planner does not rely on the names “workshop” or “shelter A”.

These facts leave four workers who meet the task's requirements, four free places and useful work for four. The immediate allocator proposes Rana, Yusuf, Dana and Bilal. Seven remain in recovery, and the trench stays held. The authored replay records Huda's four-person workshop assignment at 08:11; a calculated proposal and a recorded human assignment remain distinct records.

Rules for choosing workers and planning their breaks

The earlier immediate allocator chooses a group for one moment. The work/rest planner extends that decision across time: who can work, when each person needs recovery, and where everyone can fit. It starts with the work and recovery already recorded, so a new request cannot erase time already worked.

Every assignment must satisfy the qualification, readiness, availability, room, permit, resource and supervision rules. Within those limits, the planner first tries to fill useful workshop time. It then prefers fewer moves and work/rest changes, earlier useful work and a consistent worker-ID order. An expired confirmation cannot support work, even when the worker remains qualified.

A solve request asks the software to find a plan. A check request asks whether a supplied plan obeys the rules. A smaller plan can pass the check even when more work could be assigned. The captured full-hour responses say FEASIBLE: a valid plan was found, but the five-second run did not prove that every preference was the best possible.

Work and recovery during the next hour

At 08:11 the timed planner receives a sixty-minute request, ending at 09:11. Its observed history records recovery from 08:04 to 08:11, so the affected group has seven recorded cooled minutes. That satisfies the fictional six-minute initial requirement; the four selected workers also have their separate task-readiness confirmations.

The plan gives Rana, Yusuf, Dana and Bilal the following intervals. The seven other affected workers remain in recovery throughout the requested hour.

This is one elapsed hour, not two. Each assigned worker performs two fifteen-minute work periods: thirty workshop minutes per person. Four people × thirty minutes gives 120 useful worker-minutes. Recovery time and the two fixed workshop occupants contribute nothing to that productive total. Demand for four workers across sixty minutes is 240 worker-minutes, so the response also reports 120 unmet worker-minutes. Neither total estimates task completion time.

The schedule is stored in crew_rest.intervals. Every entry identifies a worker, activity, location, start and end. The totals summarise those entries; a client does not derive the schedule from “120”. The returned intervals are shown alongside the corresponding JSON in the examples below.

The three timing durations can now be supplied in the request’s timing_policy object. The schedule above uses the example values 6, 15 and 15. The fifteen-minute periods come from the named fictional test policy. They are not a claim about a normal or safe work/rest pattern. This demonstration plans in one-minute steps and assumes that location changes happen at their boundaries without travel time. Bilal's symptom report and the revised crew plan

At 08:18 Bilal reports symptoms, seven minutes into the first workshop period. That report excludes him from proposed work even though his earlier readiness record still says “ready”. Huda records the report and the protective response. Salim is the named first-aid responder; the planner does not diagnose Bilal or require proof of illness before excluding him.

Pending removal and first-aid contact

If removal and first-aid contact have not been confirmed, the response returns those outstanding actions and no productive schedule. Huda must confirm Bilal's removal and location, and first-aid contact must be recorded. The software does not invent those completed actions from the fact that an incident was reported.

Replanning without a confirmed replacement

Once the supplied record confirms removal to the first-aid location and contact with Salim, Bilal remains unavailable. Rana, Yusuf and Dana can be considered for continued workshop work. Their observed history already contains seven work minutes, leaving eight minutes until 08:26; a new API request does not reset their work clock. They then recover until 08:41 before the next work period.

The new sixty-minute horizon runs from 08:18 to 09:18. The captured continuation gives those three workers ninety future workshop worker-minutes in total. Their final work period ends at 09:18, and the planner reserves the required recovery through 09:33. Leila stays in recovery because her task readiness is still unknown.

Replanning with Leila's readiness confirmation

A separate branch supplies Huda's task-specific confirmation for Leila at 08:18. Her recorded recovery and qualification already meet the other eligibility checks. She can therefore be considered for the vacant place, bringing the future productive total to 120 worker-minutes in the new hour.

Leila follows her own schedule: work 08:18–08:33, recovery 08:33–08:48, work 08:48–09:03, then continued recovery. She does not inherit Bilal's clock. Her confirmation concerns her own suitability for the workshop task; it says nothing about Bilal's diagnosis or return to work. First-aid contact does not clear him to return.

Checks before a human restart decision

Workshop allocation does not reopen the trench. In the canonical 08:11 snapshot, the last civil-area reading is still the 08:00 observation. It is now older than the demo's five-minute freshness limit, so Maya must obtain a fresh local reading. A reading from another zone cannot substitute for it.

Restart evaluation is a separate request. It checks the proposed time, fresh local evidence, permit, personnel, recovery, water, crew readiness and midday restrictions. Even when all required checks are satisfied, the output is ready_for_human_review, with Rashid as the responsible reviewer. It does not release a hold automatically.

Every API call evaluates the supplied facts and returns applied: false. The location configuration and supervisor records identify their claimed source; this service does not verify identities or inspect the site. It does not store approvals or execute assignments.

Demonstration scope and evaluation questions

    
Why is an alarm not enough?
It signals the hold. It does not confirm that Huda stopped work, account for the crew, check a permitted alternative, or document the next supervisor's open decisions.

    
Why not ask a chatbot to choose?
Places, qualifications, evidence timing and rest obligations are hard limits. These solvers use explicit rules and optimisation on structured facts; no LLM is involved. A person reviews and records each real action.

    
Who approves the move and a restart?
Huda reviews and records assignments under site process. Rashid retains site safety authority for a separate restart review. The solver changes no site record.

    
What has actually been proved?
The local HTTP model returned the captured v1 allocations and checks and v2 operational decisions for synthetic cases. It has not proved field safety, worker health outcomes, payroll payment, or commercial return.

    
What would a pilot measure?
Supervisor response time, completeness of the event record, and whether workers understand the instruction and protected-rest route. A qualified safety review and a real site's current controls would govern any shadow test.
